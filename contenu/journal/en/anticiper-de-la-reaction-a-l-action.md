In a professional situation, what is deteriorating does not always show up as a problem. At first, it is often a sense of something being slightly off: the situation still works, but with less precision, less availability, less reliance on the rules that were set. Anticipating is not predicting what comes next. It means learning to read what is starting to shift before the situation closes down.

Predicting assumes that the course of a situation could be known in advance, as if behaviours, tensions or reactions followed a certain trajectory. Human situations do not work that way. They depend on the context, the rules in place, the state of the people involved, their shared history, their level of fatigue, what they understand and sometimes what they believe they understand.

Anticipating is therefore not about guessing the future. It means learning to read what may deteriorate before having to react. This nuance is essential. It keeps anticipation from being confused with distrust, constant worry or an obsessive search for risk. It is not about seeing danger everywhere. It is about perceiving what, in a given situation, is starting to lose stability, clarity or availability.

A situation only deteriorates suddenly in the eyes of those who did not pick up the weak signals. Before the apparent rupture, there were usually clues. Perhaps none of them was enough on its own to draw a conclusion. But together, they could already form a pattern. What matters is not the isolated sign. It is what that sign starts to indicate once you connect it to the context, the moment, the people present and what is really at stake in the situation.

This is where anticipation becomes a professional skill. It does not rest on a list of signals to memorise. A person speaking loudly is not necessarily aggressive. A silent person is not necessarily calm. A team that has stopped complaining is not necessarily at peace. You have to look at what is visible, but also ask what these behaviours may indicate about the level of strain, the availability of the people involved, and how far the rules, roles or limits still hold.

This reading calls for caution. Anticipating does not mean attributing an intention too quickly or attaching a label. In many situations, the error comes precisely from interpreting too fast. We think we see ill will where there is fatigue, provocation where there is a loss of bearings, calm where there is withdrawal, opposition where there is mostly incomprehension. Anticipating means distinguishing what we observe, what we interpret, and what we decide to do with it.

This distinction matters most when the pressure rises. As tension builds, people perceive less accurately, listen for shorter periods, tolerate ambiguity less and react faster. Words carry less well. Intentions get distorted. The rules are still there, but they no longer really guide behaviour. The situation sometimes keeps working on the surface, while it is already held together by invisible efforts, compensations or renunciations.

In those moments, anticipation does not yet say what must be done. It replaces neither analysis, nor choice, nor decision. But it conditions what becomes possible afterwards. Without anticipation, action risks shrinking into reaction. You arrive later in the situation, with fewer options, less finesse and less room to manoeuvre. You take the crisis head-on. Anticipating, on the contrary, preserves enough of a reading for what follows not to be dictated by urgency alone.

This capacity also depends on the state of the observer. You do not read a situation the same way depending on your own emotional involvement and physical state. Fatigue, irritation, fear, pressure or the urge to be done with it all alter perception. The more a situation affects us, the more likely we are to interpret quickly. Anticipation is therefore not only about the environment. It is also about oneself.

I often use this image with learners: use one eye to perceive the environment, and keep the other trained on yourself at all times. It says something simple, yet very demanding. Part of your attention stays open to what is happening around you: the relationship, the rules in place, behaviours, changes of pace. The other part stays available to what the situation stirs up in your own emotions: irritation, fear, impatience, the urge to answer too quickly or to force the situation. This double attention makes it easier to distinguish what truly comes from the situation from what comes from your own state at the moment you observe it.

Anticipating is therefore not an abstract skill. It concerns every context where professionals must remain able to understand, communicate, decide or act while conditions are tightening. Dealing with the public, management, training, security, reception, support, collective work under pressure: the fields vary, but the stakes remain close. The point is not to wait until the situation has deteriorated to the point of brutally narrowing the available options.

This skill does not remove tensions. It does not guarantee that a situation will remain under control. It replaces neither experience, nor the rules in place, nor decision-making. But it makes it possible not to be entirely subjected to the pace the situation imposes. It buys time, lucidity and sometimes a little space where, without it, only an immediate reaction would remain.

Anticipating, in the end, means preserving the ability to act. It is not about trying to control everything, nor claiming that everything should have been seen coming. It is about accepting that human situations rarely offer certainties, but often offer clues. Reading them accurately conditions everything else: regulation, communication, then action. Without that first reading, the situation sets the pace. With it, it remains possible to stay stable, available and able to intervene otherwise than under the sole pressure of urgency.
